# Key Tenets: Mentava-Style Early Reading + Scaffolded Phonics for a 4-Year-Old

Research notes for **Letters Are Alive** (iPad phonics/blending game). Compiled 2026-09-30.

## How to read this document

- **Provenance labels.** Each tenet is tagged:
  - **[Mentava/Hoven]**: stated by Mentava or Niels Hoven (their site, blog, his X posts, or interviews).
  - **[turing_hamster]**: stated by @turing_hamster (Alpha School / TimeBack developer; bio says "early literacy + ai").
  - **[General]**: well-established science-of-reading or learning-science practice. Mentava did not say it.
- **Verification caveat.** The research sandbox blocked direct page fetches, including mentava.com, tim.blog, Substack and x.com. All Mentava/Hoven/turing_hamster content below comes from **search-engine excerpts and snippets of those pages**, not from reading the full pages. Tenets marked **(reported/secondhand)** come from a third party (a reviewer, a podcast summary site) or from a snippet vague enough that I could not confirm it. I have **not** quoted anything verbatim unless the words appeared in a snippet. Treat paraphrases as paraphrases.
- **Not found.** I found nothing attributable to Hoven or Mentava on: stray-tap handling or touch-target sizes, a "no videos" rule, or explicit stop-sound sequencing. Those tenets are labelled [General].

## 1. Curriculum sequence & sounds

1. **Direct-instruction, systematic phonics, drawing on Orton-Gillingham and Engelmann.** Mentava describes its curriculum as "direct-instruction, phonics based" and inspired by Orton-Gillingham and Engelmann. It aims to take a child with no prior literacy instruction to independent reading. [Mentava/Hoven]
   Source: https://www.mentava.com/our-approach
2. **The scope is about 120 levels and about 2,300 activities.** The first ~20 levels cover kindergarten content. Levels 21–96 roughly cover Common Core 1st grade. The last ~25 levels cover 2nd-grade material: sight words and non-standard patterns such as soft g, *igh* and *tch*. The goal is to move the child to physical books quickly. [Mentava/Hoven]
   Source: https://www.mentava.com/our-approach
3. **Teach sounds, not letter names, and organize by the 44 phonemes.** Mentava's *Alphabet Sounds* book is ordered by sound rather than by letter. It includes digraphs (sh, ch, th) because "half of English sounds are written with more than one letter." For letters with many sounds, it teaches the **most common default sound** first. The book is recommended from about 12 months. [Mentava/Hoven]
   Source: https://www.mentava.com/blog/announcing-our-alphabet-sounds-book ; https://www.mentava.com/alphabet-sounds
4. **Letter–sound knowledge comes before blending, often by 6–12 months.** Hoven treats letter-sound pairing and blending as separate developmental skills. Children can often learn letter sounds early but may not be able to blend until 6–12 months later. *Mentava Basics* exists to drill letter/sound pairs until the child is ready to blend. [Mentava/Hoven]
   Source: https://www.mentava.com/blog/announcing-mentava-basics
5. **Screen for readiness: full sentences plus left-to-right order ("dogfish" vs "fishdog").** Mentava's rule of thumb is that the child speaks in full English sentences and can tell 🐶🐟 "dogfish" from 🐟🐶 "fishdog." It runs this emoji-order check before reading practice. Most students start around 3.5–5, the earliest around 2.5, and some not until 7. [Mentava/Hoven]
   Source: https://www.mentava.com/who-its-for ; https://www.mentava.com/readiness-check ; Tim Ferriss #882 https://tim.blog/2026/09/10/niels-hoven-mentava/ (reported/secondhand: the episode summary describes the test)
6. **Irregular ("heart") words get only the minimum memorization, anchored in phonics.** Hoven says a few words (*you, your, said*) must be memorized. Even these are "pretty close" to their spelling, so phonics still helps. Mentava has a post on teaching irregular words through structured phonics. [Mentava/Hoven] (post title verified; its full contents were not read)
   Source: https://www.mentava.com/blog/how-structured-phonics-teaches-kids-to-read-irregular-words
7. **Start with continuous sounds, add stop sounds later.** Continuous sounds (m, s, f, l, r, n, v, z, and short vowels) can be held, which makes blending easy. Stop sounds (b, d, g, k, p, t) cannot be stretched, so place them at the end of words (*mat, sat*) before the start (*tap, dog*). *100 Easy Lessons* begins with m, s, a and e, and its first words use only continuous sounds. [General / Engelmann]
   Source: https://www.whatihavelearnedteaching.com/stop-sounds-vs-continuous-sounds-need-know-difference/ ; Engelmann, Haddox & Bruner, *Teach Your Child to Read in 100 Easy Lessons* https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Teach-Your-Child-to-Read-in-100-Easy-Lessons/Siegfried-Engelmann/9780671631987
8. **Introduce visually and aurally confusable items far apart.** Separate b/d, p/q, m/n, and short e/i in the sequence. Direct Instruction avoids teaching easily confused items close together. [General / Engelmann DI]
   Source: https://www.nifdi.org/what-is-di/basic-philosophy.html
9. **Orthographic mapping is the end goal, not visual memory of word shapes.** Ehri's phases run pre-alphabetic → partial → full → consolidated alphabetic. Children store words as sight words by linking each grapheme to a phoneme. Full decoding of every word builds that store. [General / Ehri]
   Source: Ehri (2014), *Orthographic Mapping in the Acquisition of Sight Word Reading…* https://doi.org/10.1080/10888438.2013.819356
10. **Decoding is one strand of the rope; language comprehension is the other.** In Scarborough's Rope (word recognition × language comprehension) and the Simple View of Reading (D × LC), decoding without oral language does not produce understanding. [General]
    Source: https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/about-reading/articles/simple-view-reading ; https://readinguniverse.org/article/explore-teaching-topics/big-picture/understanding-the-simple-view-of-reading-and-scarboroughs-rope

## 2. Blending technique

11. **Sound out every word. Never guess from pictures or context.** Hoven's thread "What makes reading instruction good?" opens with the claim that beginning readers must be taught to sound out words. He criticizes schools that teach guessing. [Mentava/Hoven]
    Source: https://x.com/NielsHoven/status/1994081281722994841
12. **Pictures can mask non-reading.** Hoven cited a video in which a first grader "appears to read the sentence perfectly, but as soon as the pictures are…" covered, the child cannot. He argues that relying on pictures and guessing trains kids to avoid decoding. [Mentava/Hoven] (the tweet text is truncated in the snippet; the rest is paraphrased from a summary)
    Source: https://x.com/NielsHoven/status/1905289960326082595
13. **"Keep your voice ON" means continuous blending with no gaps between sounds.** Mentava contrasts choppy *Mm. Aaah. Nnn.* with connected *Mmmaaannn*. It shows the child a live waveform of their voice, and a bird or chick must fly across to its nest without falling. A pause makes it fall. Hoven reports this fixed his daughter's pausing "in a single day." [Mentava/Hoven]
    Source: https://www.mentava.com/learn-to-read-software ; https://twitter.com/NielsHoven/status/1798099680418832612 (reported/secondhand: the "single day" claim came via a search summary)
14. **Say it slow, then say it fast.** Engelmann's routine has the child say the sounds slowly without stopping, then "say it fast" to produce the word. The printed arrow and balls under each word pace the finger: hold continuous sounds for about 2 seconds and move quickly past stop sounds. [General / Engelmann]
    Source: *100 Easy Lessons* (above); sample at https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/100-easy-lessons-revised/34100176
15. **Blending is harder than letter knowledge and needs its own practice.** Hoven faults edutainment apps for teaching fuzzy "foundational skills" but not "the nitty gritty of actually blending sounds together into words." [Mentava/Hoven]
    Source: https://x.com/NielsHoven/status/1994081281722994841
16. **Critique to weigh: letters-first blending can mask weak phonemic awareness.** A hostile reviewer argues that Mentava always shows letters during blending. Some children then lean on letter knowledge and never build oral phoneme skills (blending, segmenting, manipulating with eyes closed). The reviewer calls this a risk for later difficulties. This is contested and comes from a competitor-style site, but adding some audio-only phoneme work is cheap insurance. (reported/secondhand, critic)
    Source: https://picturesareforbabies.com/blog/reviews/mentava/

## 3. Mastery & review

17. **Mastery-gated pace: fast kids go fast, slow kids go slow.** Mentava's core philosophy is to "let kids who can learn fast learn fast, and let kids who learn slower learn slower." The child moves ahead "as they master each skill." [Mentava/Hoven]
    Source: https://www.mentava.com/blog/how-to-let-kids-learn-really-fast/ ; https://www.mentava.com/parents
18. **Expected dose: roughly 50–200 hours.** In a 30-family pilot, Hoven reports about 50 hours (≈30 min/day for 3 months) to take near-3-year-olds to an early 2nd-grade level. Elsewhere he frames the whole job as 100–200 hours of practice. Plan for many months of content, not weeks. [Mentava/Hoven]
    Source: https://www.mentava.com/blog/how-to-let-kids-learn-really-fast/ ; https://x.com/NielsHoven/status/2001454107383734309
19. **Enforce mastery of prerequisites with accuracy and fluency.** Alpha School's app-selection principles, as described by turing_hamster: enforce mastery (don't advance until prerequisites are done with high accuracy **and** fluency), prefer direct instruction, use spaced repetition, break skills down very granularly to avoid overloading working memory, and maximize learning per minute. [turing_hamster]
    Source: https://x.com/turing_hamster/status/1972677225573990476
20. **Spaced, interleaved review beats blocked practice.** Math Academy's model: a prerequisite graph, spaced repetition, and interleaved (mixed) review. Blocked practice of one skill "can give a false sense of mastery and fluency." Choose the next task to maximize learning per unit time. [General / Math Academy]
    Source: https://www.mathacademy.com/how-our-ai-works ; https://www.justinmath.com/files/the-math-academy-way.pdf
21. **Firm-up and immediate error correction.** In Direct Instruction, errors are corrected immediately with model → lead → test, then the item is retested later ("delayed test"). Tasks are designed so the child succeeds most of the time. [General / Engelmann DI]
    Source: https://www.nifdi.org/what-is-di/basic-philosophy.html
22. **Critique to weigh: "adaptive" can just mean a linear list.** A critic notes that Mentava's curriculum is a linear list of exercises done at the child's own pace. Genuine adaptivity (diagnosing *which* sound is weak) is a place a home-built game can do better. (reported/secondhand, critic)
    Source: https://picturesareforbabies.com/blog/reviews/mentava/

## 4. Practice design / reps

23. **The activity loop is short, varied, and ends in real text.** A parent reviewer describes a typical lesson: "open the barn door and learn the sound" → tap running sheep → word blending → short stories. There is also a listen-and-choose activity (lead the rooster to the word said aloud), which that child liked least. (reported/secondhand)
    Source: https://www.dcoates.com/posts/mentava-review/
24. **Real mental effort, not "intellectual junk food."** Parents describe Mentava as tiring in a good way: active and challenging. Mentava contrasts itself with "babysitting without guilt" apps that are mostly repackaged video with minimal interactivity. [Mentava/Hoven] plus parent reports (reported/secondhand)
    Source: https://www.mentava.com/blog/false-promises-the-reality-behind-educational-reading-apps/ ; https://medium.com/@jacqueline.momoh22/why-a-500-month-childs-reading-app-caught-my-eye-f3513d4e77b7
25. **Keep sessions short and daily: 15–30 focused minutes, ending when enough focused practice is done.** Mentava says most families practice about 15–30 minutes a day. Sessions end on effort, not on the clock. Mentava says it "only succeeds with daily practice." [Mentava/Hoven]
    Source: https://www.mentava.com/parents
26. **Every rep requires the target skill.** Mentava positions itself against apps where kids can progress by "tapping blindly." Design each item so it cannot be solved without decoding: no picture cues present during decoding, and distractors that differ by a single phoneme. [Mentava/Hoven framing (reported/secondhand) + General]
    Source: https://www.mentava.com/blog/false-promises-the-reality-behind-educational-reading-apps/
27. **Minimize working-memory load.** Break skills into the smallest steps, and introduce one new thing at a time among mostly known items (DI's "mostly review, a little new"). [turing_hamster + General]
    Source: https://x.com/turing_hamster/status/1972677225573990476 ; Rosenshine, *Principles of Instruction* https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf

## 5. Touch-screen & UI design for young kids

Mentava's multisensory feedback is documented: its tutor "uses audio, visual, and tactile feedback," and it has the voice-waveform bird (tenet 13). Beyond that, I found **no** published Hoven guidance on stray taps or touch targets. The items below are general child-UX practice, apart from 28.

28. **Make an abstract skill visible and physical.** The waveform plus a chick crossing a gap turns "don't pause" into something a 3-year-old can see. The underlying principle: give instant, concrete, non-verbal feedback on exactly the sub-skill being trained. [Mentava/Hoven]
    Source: https://www.mentava.com/learn-to-read-software
29. **Use big targets and generous spacing.** Nielsen Norman Group recommends touch targets for young children of at least about 2 cm × 2 cm (≈4× the adult minimum), with space between them. Young children also struggle with precise drags and multi-touch. [General]
    Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/children-ux-physical-development/
30. **Tolerate palms and accidental touches. Don't penalize imprecision.** Resting palms and the other hand cause stray touches. Ignore touches outside active zones, use the first intentional touch, debounce repeated taps, and never count a mis-touch as a wrong answer. Keep system gestures and exits out of reach (Guided Access). [General]
    Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/children-ux-physical-development/ ; https://developers.google.com/building-for-kids/designing-engaging-apps
31. **Audio instructions, no reading required.** Pre-readers need every prompt spoken, re-playable with a big "hear it again" button, and never time-limited. [General]
    Source: https://developers.google.com/building-for-kids/designing-engaging-apps
32. **Make left-to-right physical.** Engelmann's arrow and finger slide, and the drag in your frog mechanic, both build directionality. Mentava treats directionality as a readiness gate (tenet 5). Motion should always go left → right, with the sound starting as the finger reaches each letter. [General + Mentava readiness framing]
    Source: https://www.mentava.com/who-its-for ; *100 Easy Lessons*
33. **Keep screens clean and cut decorative motion.** Every animated or tappable decoration pulls attention and invites random taps. During a learning item, only the relevant elements should be interactive. [General]
    Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/children-ux-physical-development/

## 6. Motivation & gamification

34. **Education = curriculum + motivation, and motivation is the hard part.** Hoven says we already know *how* to teach reading (phonics). What most software gets wrong is getting a small child to sit through the repetition blending requires. [Mentava/Hoven]
    Source: https://www.mentava.com/our-approach ; https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-niels-hiven-of-mentava/ ; https://www.thefabulous.co/pod/tim-ferriss/mentava-reading-app-does-the-500-method-work/ (reported/secondhand)
35. **"Lower the hoop" rather than only "raise the reward."** In Hoven's metaphor, you can make the reward bigger or make the jump easier. He borrows from slot machines: an enticing reward for a very easy action. For learning, lower the hoop through UX that removes friction and makes each step small enough to succeed. [Mentava/Hoven] (via the Tim Ferriss #882 summary; reported/secondhand)
    Source: https://tim.blog/2026/09/10/niels-hoven-mentava/
36. **Game-style motivation layered on top of the curriculum, never replacing it.** Hoven ran the top-grossing mobile game of 2011 (Pocket Gems). Mentava "layers game-style motivation design" on the phonics so kids want to practice without a parent acting as teacher. The game serves the reps and never substitutes for them. [Mentava/Hoven]
    Source: https://www.mentava.com/niels ; https://tim.blog/2026/09/10/niels-hoven-mentava/ (reported/secondhand)
37. **Guard against edutainment drift.** Mentava argues that most kids' reading apps sell "babysitting without guilt." The longer they hold attention, the better they deliver that promise, so engagement time is the wrong metric. Optimize learning per minute, not minutes. [Mentava/Hoven + turing_hamster]
    Source: https://www.mentava.com/blog/false-promises-the-reality-behind-educational-reading-apps/ ; https://x.com/turing_hamster/status/1972677225573990476
38. **Motivation beats the app choice.** An Alpha-adjacent writer summarizes: "Don't focus on the app, focus on the motivation." Alpha pairs a 2-hour focused block with external incentives. [General / Alpha School, reported/secondhand]
    Source: https://austinscholar.substack.com/p/austin-scholar-177-the-alpha-app

## 7. Parent role

39. **The software teaches; the parent encourages, sets the routine, and celebrates.** Mentava "leads with instruction, feedback, and pacing." The parent's job is the daily habit, encouragement through hard parts, and celebration. The parent is not the answer-giver. [Mentava/Hoven]
    Source: https://www.mentava.com/parents ; https://www.mentava.com/blog/how-to-let-kids-learn-really-fast/
40. **A cheap alternative works if the parent supplies the motivation.** Hoven recommends *Teach Your Child to Read in 100 Easy Lessons* ($17) as "excellent," with a long track record and "the best layman's explanation of how to teach a kid." The catch is that the parent must keep the child engaged. He says it is what worked for his own daughter, done at bedtime together. [Mentava/Hoven]
    Source: https://x.com/NielsHoven/status/1993459046431539687 ; https://x.com/NielsHoven/status/1782548839599395079
41. **Parental presence still matters in practice.** Reviewers note that a parent often needs to sit nearby, especially at first, to keep the child going through repetitive stretches. (reported/secondhand)
    Source: https://www.dcoates.com/posts/mentava-review-follow-up/

## 8. Vocabulary, oral language & the word gap (turing_hamster + general)

I found very little turing_hamster reading content through search. The account's bio is "early literacy + ai" and it works on Alpha School / TimeBack. Only the items below could be attributed.

42. **Talk to your child: language input is a gift.** turing_hamster posted about Hart & Risley's "30 Million Word Gap," calling it among the most-cited early-childhood studies. The post framed talking to your child as "one of the best gifts you can give your child." (reported/secondhand: seen only as a search summary; the tweet URL was not found)
    Source: https://x.com/turing_hamster
43. **The 30M figure is contested, but the direction holds.** Replications (e.g., Sperry et al., 2018) dispute the magnitude and the SES framing. Measures of *conversational turns* and *child-directed speech* still predict vocabulary and language outcomes. Shared book reading adds rare words that talk alone does not supply (the "million word gap" from reading aloud). [General]
    Source: https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/is-there-really-a-30-million-word-gap ; https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2019/04/190404074947.htm
44. **Mentava is used inside Alpha School's reading stack.** turing_hamster lists Mentava, Lalilo, FastPhonics, Alpha Read, ClearFluency, Anton and others among Alpha's most-used apps. Decoding apps sit alongside fluency and comprehension apps. [turing_hamster]
    Source: https://x.com/turing_hamster/status/1972744047581368324
45. **Decodable words must be words the child knows.** A child can decode *vat* or *sod* and still not know they are words. Blending only "clicks" when the output matches a word in the child's oral vocabulary, so pick early decodable words from a 4-year-old's spoken vocabulary. Use read-alouds, with dialogic questions, to grow the language-comprehension strand in parallel. [General]
    Source: https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/about-reading/articles/simple-view-reading

## Implications for Letters Are Alive

Current design: 17 levels; continuous sounds first (m/a/s/t); most-common sound only until magic-e at L14; egg sound→letter matching; frog drag left→right across lily pads with each sound playing, then a picture choice; spelling by tapping letters; sentence reading with heart words; 90% first-try mastery gates; spaced review; stars/animals/farm-shop rewards; sing-along songs.

**Already strongly aligned:** sound-first letters; most-common-sound-first; continuous-first start; physical left-to-right blending; mastery gates; spaced review; heart words kept minimal; games layered on the curriculum. Keep all of these.

### Curriculum & sequence
1. **Reconsider /t/ in the first four.** /t/ is a stop sound. Use it only in word-final position (*at, sat, mat*) until blending is solid, and delay initial stops (*tam, tap*) by a level or two. Consider m, s, a, then f/l/n/r before t. (Tenet 7)
2. **Add a pre-level-1 readiness mini-game (Mentava's "dogfish/fishdog").** Show 🐶🐟 vs 🐟🐶 and ask for "fish dog," across ~5 trials. If the child fails, stay in a letter-sounds-only mode ("Basics") with egg matching and songs, and retest weekly. (Tenets 4–5)
3. **Separate confusable letters** (b/d, p/q, m/n, e/i) across levels, and add explicit discrimination items when the second one arrives. (Tenet 8)
4. **Seventeen levels is probably only a Mentava-K-equivalent slice.** Mentava uses ~20 levels for kindergarten and ~120 in total. Plan for digraphs (sh, ch, th, ck), blends (CCVC/CVCC), and then vowel teams after magic-e. Use Mentava's 44-sound framing. (Tenets 2–3, 18)
5. **Build decodable word lists only from words a 4-year-old says.** Avoid *vat, sod, nab*. (Tenet 45)

### Blending mechanic (frog on lily pads)
6. **Continuous sound across pads, not discrete clips.** The biggest Mentava-specific insight is "keep your voice ON." Instead of playing isolated sounds per pad, stretch the *current* sound for as long as the finger stays on the pad and crossfade into the next. The frog's drag then *is* the slow blend (*mmmaaat*). Then auto-play "say it fast" (*mat*) at the last pad. For stop sounds, make the pad short or "bouncy" so the finger moves on quickly. (Tenets 13–14)
7. **Optional voice mode.** If you add mic input, a simple energy-envelope check (no speech recognition needed) can detect gaps: the frog "falls in the water" if the child's voice drops between sounds. This replicates Mentava's chick. Keep it a parent-enabled mode, because false negatives frustrate. (Tenet 13)
8. **Hide the picture until after decoding.** Picture choices must appear only after the child finishes the drag, and must not be guessable from the first sound alone. Use foils that differ by one phoneme in any position (*mat / map / mop*), or the child will learn to guess from the first letter. (Tenets 11–12, 26)
9. **Add a "read it without the frog" fade.** Once a word pattern is mastered, show the word and ask the child to tap the matching picture *without* the sound-by-sound playback. Mastery should mean reading, not listening to the app blend. Later, add timed or fluency-flavored rounds (accuracy **and** fluency). (Tenet 19)
10. **Add a few audio-only phoneme items** (e.g., "which picture is /m/-/o/-/p/?" with no letters shown, and "what's the first sound in *sun*?"). This hedges against the "letters mask weak phonemic awareness" critique. (Tenet 16)

### Mastery, review, error handling
11. **Keep 90% first-try gates, but add a fluency criterion and a delayed retest.** For example, the item must also be answered within N seconds, and it is re-asked 1+ sessions later before counting as mastered. (Tenets 19–21)
12. **Error correction: model → lead → test → delayed retest.** On a miss, play the correct blend, have the child redo the drag, then re-insert that item 2–4 items later. Don't just show a red X and move on. (Tenet 21)
13. **Interleave review across levels** rather than running a review block. Mix old words into every new-level session (roughly 80% known, 20% new). (Tenets 20, 27)
14. **Track per-grapheme accuracy,** not just per-level, so review targets the weak sound (e.g., /e/ vs /i/). This makes it more adaptive than a linear list. (Tenet 22)

### Touch-screen UI
15. **Targets ≥ 2 cm (~75–90 pt on iPad), widely spaced.** Make the lily-pad drag forgiving: snap to the nearest pad, allow wobble, and never fail the child for leaving the path. (Tenet 29)
16. **Stray-tap hygiene.** Use single-touch only (ignore extra touches and palms), debounce, and lock input while audio prompts play. Don't count a tap made during a prompt or animation as an answer. Only elements in the current task should be interactive. Recommend Guided Access to the parent. (Tenets 30, 33)
17. **Every instruction is spoken and has a big replay button. No time pressure before mastery.** (Tenet 31)

### Motivation
18. **Rewards are fine, but pay for reps, not time.** Stars and animals should scale with correct first-try decodes and completed reviews, never with minutes played. Farm-shop spending should never interrupt a practice set mid-stream. (Tenets 36–37)
19. **"Lower the hoop."** Measure where the child stalls (hesitation, repeated misses) and shrink that step: fewer choices, a shorter word, more modelling. Don't just add bigger rewards. (Tenet 35)
20. **Keep songs as a warm-up or reward, not as the lesson.** Sing-alongs support letter-sound memory and fun, but keep them out of the timed practice block so the session stays active rather than passive. (Tenets 24, 37)
21. **A daily session target of about 10–15 minutes for a 4-year-old.** Mentava recommends 15–30. End on effort (e.g., after N successful reps) with a clear "all done today!" screen, rather than allowing open-ended play. (Tenet 25)

### Parent & language
22. **Parent dashboard plus scripts.** Show per-sound mastery. Give the parent a one-line role: "sit nearby, cheer, don't give answers." Add a weekly "read this real book together" nudge once sentence reading starts, since Mentava aims to transition to physical books. (Tenets 2, 39–41)
23. **Language strand outside the game.** Add "talk about it" prompts: after a word like *map*, show a parent prompt ("Ask: where would we go with a map?"). Recommend daily read-alouds, which the game can't replace. (Tenets 42–45)
